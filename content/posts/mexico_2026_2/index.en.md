I arrived at the National Museum of Anthropology on a noisy, sun-drenched afternoon. Outside the doors, the sounds of traffic and people crowded together; after entering, the heat seemed to have been left outside the glass doors, and in the inner courtyard, only the sound of water remained. Tourists scattered at the entrance, then were slowly drawn towards the massive pillar reaching from the ground to the ceiling in the center. Water fell continuously from the edge of the roof, like a pruned rainforest. On the pillar's relief, a large tree extended its branches, and at the crown grew a complex face, half Indigenous and half Spanish.

{{< figure src="入口水帘图腾.jpg" alt="入口水帘图腾" caption="Water curtain totem at the entrance" >}}

Tourists stopped from time to time to take photos in front of it. I watched the Mexicans walking past the water curtain; some had darker skin, some had higher nose bridges, and the eye sockets and smiles of others carried traces of another origin. That kind of mixing didn't require explanation; it was just there on their faces, growing naturally. In contrast, as an outsider, I felt a bit clumsy standing there, eager to find a suitable adjective for everything before my eyes.

I followed the arrows into the exhibition halls. I quickly confused the names, dates, and deities in those display cases. What truly made me stop was a completely unfamiliar order. Sun, rain, corn, death, and war had all been earnestly explained by another civilization. They weren't like the gods in Greek mythology, who were prone to jealousy, love, and temper tantrums, nor were they like the stories in the Bible, which always have a thread of sin and redemption. The gods here were closer to the earth, like a set of coarse rules: why the sky brightens, why crops grow, why death must be arranged into life.

I thought of the Aztec legend of the "Five Suns," where the world was created and destroyed time and time again. The story sounded distant, but standing in the exhibition hall, it wasn't abstract. Those stone carvings were like things left over from several destructions, polished, numbered, and placed in glass cases. Tourists walked past with light steps, their phone screens lighting up and then dimming again. A vanished world hadn't truly vanished; it just changed its form, surrounded and observed by us.

In the Mexica hall, I stopped in front of the earth goddess Coatlicue. she had almost no relation to the female sculptures I was familiar with. She wasn't soft, quiet, or trying to please the viewer. Snakes intertwined to form her skirt, human hands and hearts hung on her chest, and two facing snakes replaced her head. She was more like the black root system exposed when the earth had just cracked open, primal, wild, carrying the smell of dirt and blood. Standing in front of her, what I felt was a coarse oppression, a power that could give birth, swallow, destroy, and grow anew.

{{< figure src="大地女神科阿特利库埃（Coatlicue）.jpg" alt="Coatlicue" caption="Earth Goddess Coatlicue" >}}

The Sun Stone was not far away, suspended high in the center of the hall, with tourists holding their phones lining up in front of it. I didn't remember the details of its calendar, only that kind of weight. The ancient world didn't seem to believe that order would happen naturally: the sun rising, rain falling, corn ripening all required offerings, even blood. This idea was cruel, but not as distant as I imagined. I had also seen another sun in Silicon Valley. Screens flashing with code stayed lit all night, and people talked about layoffs, visas, and whether AI would replace them, like discussing a weather system that had already gathered overhead. I was in it too, handing over my time, body, and anxiety to some invisible order. Perhaps hundreds or thousands of years later, another civilization looking at us in front of a display case would also be confused: why did these people spend their whole lives sitting in front of glowing screens, and why did they organize insurance, loans, performance reviews, and news push notifications so tightly? Thinking of this, I suddenly felt an untimely intimacy with that stone.

When I left the museum, the water in the inner courtyard was still falling, and tourists were still taking photos. I suddenly understood why some people call these civilizations "alien civilizations": it was indeed strange and baffling. But this metaphor was also too lazy, like sticking a label of "incomprehensible" on an unfamiliar thing, and then safely bypassing it. It wasn't on an alien planet, but under the lakebed of Mexico City today, under the streets, churches, subways, and stalls. A person inherited the face of the conquered, and also inherited the language of the conqueror. When he says "we," is there a crack in his voice?

The next day, I joined a historical walking tour, gathering at the Zócalo (Constitution Square). The square was huge; standing on one side, you could barely see the buildings on the other. In the middle rose a massive Mexican flag, and when the wind blew, the whole piece of cloth moved like a wall. To the east was the National Palace, to the north was the Metropolitan Cathedral, and to the northeast was the Templo Mayor ruins. The tour guide was a woman in her forties. When talking about the Mexica and Tenochtitlan, her speaking speed would quicken, and her hands would gesture along. She took me and a group of American tourists—common everywhere in Mexico City—stopping station by station around the edge of the square.

The guide said that Aztec and Mexica temples were often built layer upon layer, the new covering the old. Later, the Spanish tore down the temples and moved the stones to build churches and palaces. When she talked about how power workers in 1978, while constructing near the Cathedral, dug up the circular stone disk of Coyolxauhqui, she paused, as if deliberately giving us a little time to imagine that scene: the workers were originally just there to fix cables, but with one shovel down, the city handed up a piece of a god's body from underground. The past didn't always return solemnly; sometimes it just startled the construction crew. Listening from the side, I felt that the Zócalo also resembled a larger, layered altar. At the very bottom were the Aztec ruins, stones lying low on the ground, like suppressed bones; in the middle were colonial-era churches and colleges; and further up, twentieth-century public buildings. They didn't make way for each other, but simply crowded onto the same piece of land.

We later walked into the Metropolitan Cathedral. It was built on the lakebed; the stones were too heavy, the land too soft, and to this day, it is still sinking. The guide told us to pay attention to the floor and wall lines, and only then did I notice that those vertical lines, originally meant to draw the eye upward, had a slight deviation near the ground. That tilt was very subtle, not enough to be frightening, but there was a calmness before a building's collapse. The church didn't show panic; it still opened its doors, lit its lamps, and let people in to pray.

Inside, Mass was taking place. The sound of prayers, passing through the stone pillars, the golden altar, and the pipe organ, became blurred and docile. People with lowered gazes sat on the benches; a woman placed her handbag on her lap, her fingers slowly rubbing the strap. Next to her, a man had his eyes closed, looking as if he had fallen asleep, but also as if he was praying earnestly. I stood in the side aisle, unable to see where the priest was, only seeing light falling from above, pausing for a moment on the edge of a stone pillar, and then slowly dimming. The honking outside continued, but here, it sounded as if separated by a thick cloth.

The guide led us down the side aisle to look behind a railing at a life-size statue of Christ. That was the Señor del Cacao (Lord of Cacao). He was different from the crucifixions I had seen before; his expression was very vivid, tilting his head and resting it on his hand, looking a bit tired, like an exhausted office worker after a long day. In front of the Christ statue sat a jar filled with cacao beans. The guide said that cacao beans were once a valuable currency in Mesoamerica, and the Indigenous people would use them as money. The new religion first accepted the money of the old world, and then slowly entered the life of the old world. This method was truly shrewd: not rushing to make people forget the original things, but first collecting the original things onto its own plate. Hearing this, the Americans in the group showed an expression of being personally offended by the commercial acumen of five hundred years ago. I looked at that small jar and thought these Spaniards sure knew how to do business.

After coming out of the Cathedral, I began to notice more of this mixture. The image of the Virgin of Guadalupe appeared at stalls and storefronts; she wore a starry cloak, stepped on the moon, and was often surrounded by a halo of radiating light. She didn't look like a European holy icon from afar, but more like someone who had lived locally for a long time.

{{< figure src="融合了瓜达卢佩圣母的教皇.jpg" alt="Virgin of Guadalupe at a street stall" caption="Street stall with the image of the Virgin of Guadalupe" >}}

Later, passing by several churches, the facades were full of gold, scrollwork, angels, and patterns, layer pressing upon layer, leaving almost no room for blank space. Mexican Baroque had a bustling sense of excess, as if not trusting the sacred to shine on its own, insisting on pasting another ring of gold leaf around it.

{{< figure src="墨西哥巴洛克.jpg" alt="Mexican Baroque" caption="Complex Mexican Baroque facade" >}}

Approaching noon, the square gradually grew noisy. Someone wearing a feather headdress, with jingling ornaments tied to their ankles and bare arms, was performing a purification ritual on the edge of the square. A drum beat steadily, bundles of grass swept over the tourists' shoulders, heads, and backs, and smoke swirled around their faces. On a plastic sheet nearby, holy icons, amulets, phone cases, and keychains were laid out. A little girl, hugging a water bottle almost half her size, walked unsteadily, the water inside sloshing with her steps. I watched her weave through the crowd, unable to help but sweat for her, afraid that the bottle of water would spill on the square before Mexican history did.

Beneath the colonial-era buildings surrounding the square were rows of stalls. The iron griddle of a taco stand sent up oily smoke, with chopped onions, cilantro, and hot sauce in clear plastic containers. Someone was selling cotton candy, someone else carried a whole row of balloons on their back, walking like they were dragging a bright cloud. Children's Day was approaching, and kids darted through the crowd holding plastic toys. The words about conquest, mixed blood, and religious transformation had barely settled in my mind before they were shattered by a child's scream. Standing at the edge of the flag's shadow, I suddenly felt that the most impressive thing here wasn't how well it preserved history, but how it always managed to place a bottle of cola, a bunch of balloons, and a plate of sizzling meat right next to it.

After dark, I was eating tacos and churros by the roadside again. The meat was sliced straight off the rotating spit; it was still hot when handed to me, and before long, the blue tortilla was softened by the meat juices. The churro was like a sugar-coated fried dough stick, still steaming inside when bitten. I stood by the stall, trying to stuff everything into my mouth, the sauce dripping all the way down to my wrist. The vendor handed me a bottle of hot sauce; I was about to pour it on the taco, but unexpectedly, he motioned for me to hold out the back of my hand. He dripped two kinds of hot sauce onto the back of my hand, gesturing for me to taste them first before adding them to the taco. Each one was very spicy. Unfortunately, I wasn't an American who couldn't handle spice; I boldly added a huge pile to my taco, even feeling a bit smug. The next second, I started gasping, "sss-haa," my nose sweating, searching everywhere for a tissue. The vendor watched me and laughed, and I had to laugh too. That bit of confidence about "being able to eat spicy food" was quickly proven to be just another tourist illusion.

Walking along, I didn't expect Mexico City to have a Chinatown as well. The characters on the archway were in a neat, bold font, looking as if they were generated directly from a printer, so earnest it was a bit comical. Red lanterns hung across the street, and shops sold Maneki-neko, sushi, paper umbrellas, and paper hats that looked like conical bamboo hats. There were hardly any Chinese faces among the people soliciting business on the street. It was a weekend night, and many young Mexicans were cheerfully walking around wearing the paper hats. The scene looked like someone had forcefully pieced together a small commodities market, the Spring Festival Gala, and a Latin American street; it was joyful in its crudeness, and surprisingly, it didn't fall apart.

{{< figure src="墨西哥城中国城.jpg" alt="Mexico City Chinatown" caption="Mexico City's Chinatown decorated with red lanterns" >}}

At one end of Chinatown was a bar. I ordered a tequila, and it came with three shot glasses: tequila in the middle, flanked by a glass of lime juice and a glass of tomato juice, meant to be mixed. Someone behind the bar sang softly along to the music. There, I met two American girls who taught at an international school in Monterrey. The younger one said she changed countries every few years, and even lived in Hangzhou for three years during the pandemic. Later, she wanted to be closer to home but didn't want to give up the lucrative pay of an international school, so she moved to Monterrey, where a five-hour drive north would take her back to her parents' home in Houston. The other woman looked to be in her forties, but her eyes often burst with a naive and eager light. She said she didn't actually like teaching; facing over twenty kids every day, she only wanted to lie down when she got home. But the job had allowed her to visit over sixty countries. When she was younger, she also thought about getting married, having kids, and settling down, but now she had fallen in love with her life.

"I don't know if that's selfish," she said. "But for the first time, I feel like my life doesn't need to be explained to anyone." After she said that, she smiled and pushed her glass towards the inside of the bar, asking the bartender for a refill.

On the way back, I was still thinking about what those two girls said. I envied them a little, and it reminded me of my childhood dream to travel the world. That envy wasn't a desire to quit my job immediately and go wandering, but rather a sudden realization that a person's life really can have many different arrangements. Having stayed in Silicon Valley for a long time, it's easy to think of life as a roadmap: school, job, visa status, rent, promotion—every step feeling like it needs to be reviewed by someone else. But that night, Mexico City was like a map unrolled at my feet: noisy, chaotic, with branching paths everywhere. I thought of the middle school girls I met in Nukus, Uzbekistan; when they heard I flew in from the US and had been to so many places, their eyes suddenly lit up. That kind of gaze made me a bit embarrassed. Work did take away a lot of my time, but it also gave me a bit of financial freedom, allowing me to stay in hostels, eat at street stalls, and come to a square like this, to be spun around and dazzled by a city.

When that day ended, everything about Mexico City was crowded into my head. The sound of water at the Anthropology Museum, Coatlicue's snake skirt, the sinking Cathedral, the Cacao Christ, the smoke in the square, the children's toys at the stalls, the red lanterns in Chinatown, and the girl's words—they all remained in their respective places. They didn't line up neatly, nor did they obediently help me prove anything. Heading back at night, the soles of my shoes still carried the dust from the square, and the air smelled of sugar, chili peppers, hot oil, and wet stones after the rain. I thought, perhaps this city doesn't need to be defined. Every evening, it simply takes the things that have been destroyed, moved, misread, and redecorated, and displays them all over again, bustling and lively.
